Why the next decade of retail belongs to places, not just stores

Share this article

  • LinkedIn
  • Facebook

Shoppers can buy almost anything from a phone. What they cannot download is a place worth spending an afternoon in, and that is changing what owners ask of their retail assets.

For most of the past thirty years a mall was judged by its anchors and its occupancy. Both still matter. But across the region, the destinations that are growing footfall and sales are the ones that give people a reason to come that has little to do with a shopping list.

That reason is rarely one big attraction. It is the sum of many small decisions: how the ground floor meets the street, where people can sit without buying anything, which local operators sit beside the international names, and what happens in the atrium on a Tuesday night.

From tenant mix to destination mix

Curation used to mean filling categories. Today it means composing a day: breakfast, a class, an errand, a meeting, dinner. When a destination supports the whole day, visits get longer and more frequent, and tenants trade better together than they would apart.

"The question is no longer what we can lease, but what people will come back for."

This asks more of an operator. Programming, service and data have to work as one, and the results have to be measured in dwell time and repeat visits as well as rent.

For owners, the reward is resilience. A place people choose is harder to replace than a store they can find anywhere, and it holds its value through changes in what is sold inside it.
